# Examples: real before and after

All three come from work on [idunworks.com](https://idunworks.com/blog): two from drafts the owner edited before publishing, one from a page the owner sent back. None is invented. Quotes are short and from published posts.

## A. A true text that said nothing of its own

A post about writing a community's promises to its members. The draft was correct, sentence by sentence, and flat.

| Draft | Published |
|---|---|
| "The first draft about public groups was honest." | "…was serious." |
| The risks listed: "AI reading, bots that ignore requests, Google." | "AI reading for their training, bots…, Google, yadayada." |
| A paragraph closing on "The order is part of the honesty." | "…the honesty, in a way." |
| (nothing) | "Besides, who reads to the end?" |

**Clues:** a list with no stakes (reading *for what*?), a tidy moral closing the paragraph, no voice anywhere. **What changed:** one word says what the risk actually is, a shrug and a rhetorical question put a person back in, and the neat conclusion admits it's only partly true. None of it needed a new fact; it needed someone who cares to be audible.

**A review would have said:** "'AI reading': reading for what? Name the risk." And: "This paragraph ends on a moral. Is it fully true? If not, say how far."

## B. A claim with no cost in it

A post about a small browser game built from one message. The draft's lead was a placeholder about "the one check no test can do", and the body said, correctly, "Twenty-six minutes later there was a commit."

The owner added, right after it: "Damn! Cost? Some server time and 10 minutes of mine." The lead became a question: "Simple enough, or is it?"

**Clues:** a confident fact with no real-world detail around it (what did it cost the person?), and an abstract lead. **What changed:** a firsthand number and a reaction. The reader now knows what the claim means for them.

**A review would have said:** "Twenty-six minutes of whose time? The open question is the author's: what did this actually cost you?" Not: "add more detail".

## C. A page where everything shouted

A community page in a loud brutalist theme: thick frames and hard offset shadows. Every control had them: the small ✕ and ⋯ on each post as much as the main "Post" button, a single action as much as a whole card. The owner's verdict: the hierarchy was "non-existent", and the members' own text was the quietest thing on the page.

**What changed:** the theme stayed, scaled by importance. Buttons inside posts and comments went quiet (32 px, a 1 px frame, still a 44 px hit area for fingers). Only "Comment" and "Post" kept the full frame and the brand yellow, as the one main action in their box. Member text went up a size, names and dates below it.

**Clues:** everything loud, so nothing scans; a style fighting its content. **Who it helps, what task, evidence:** a member reading a thread (the task) now meets the post first and the actions after; the evidence was the owner looking at the live page and finding no hierarchy.

**A review would have said:** "On a phone, which do you see first: the post, or its five buttons? Make one action per box full weight and quiet the rest; keep the hit areas."
