# Text: clues and fixes

Each clue is a place to look harder. It becomes a finding only when you can quote the line and say what would be better. For how to write in a particular owner's voice from the start, see the `session-to-blog` skill's `references/voice.md`; this page is for reviewing what's already written.

## Generic claims

**Looks like:** "In today's fast-paced world", "more important than ever", "a game-changer", "unlock the power of", "seamless", "robust". An opening that would fit any post on any topic.

**Fix:** start where the work started: the day, the error, the number, the person who noticed. Cut the sentence and see if anything is lost. Usually nothing is.

## The same point, again and again

**Looks like:** the intro says the lesson, each section restates it, and the outro says it once more ("In short, …", "The takeaway: …"). The reader is told three times and shown once.

**Fix:** say it once, where it's earned, usually after the story that proves it. Let the outro add something the post hasn't said: a trade-off, an alternative, what's still open.

## Vague evidence

**Looks like:** "studies show", "experts agree", "it's widely known", "many teams find", "significantly faster".

**Fix:** name the source and link it, or give your own measurement with its setup ("on a 2019 laptop, cold cache, 1.9 s to 0.4 s"), or say plainly that it's your impression. If there is no source, the claim goes or turns into an opinion.

## Over-structured rhythm

**Looks like:** every section is a claim, three bullets and a tidy summary line. Lists of exactly three. Every paragraph the same length. Headings in matching grammar ("Understanding X", "Exploring Y", "Embracing Z").

**Fix:** vary it. A one-line paragraph. A list of two, or of five. A section that's just a story. Keep bullets for things that really are parallel: steps, options, a checklist.

## No real-world detail

**Looks like:** nothing could have gone differently. No constraint, no trade-off, no thing that took three tries, no exception, no firsthand observation, nobody named.

**Fix:** ask the author (don't invent): what broke first, what it cost in time or money, what you'd do differently, who tested it, which case it still doesn't handle. One true detail does more than a paragraph of framing.

## Confident specifics nobody checked

**Looks like:** an exact number, a quote with a famous name on it, a version, a date, a law or a feature claim, stated flatly with no source and no test behind it.

**Fix:** check it, link it, or soften it to what's known. A quote's attribution gets checked before it stays. Wrong specifics are worse than vague ones, because they read as true.

## Read it aloud

**Listen for:**

- **too many transitions:** "Moreover", "Furthermore", "That said", "Ultimately", "It's worth noting that";
- **inflated adjectives:** "crucial", "powerful", "incredibly", "truly", "vital", piled up;
- **tidy conclusions:** a paragraph ending on a neat moral ("And that's the real lesson."), or the whole post closing on one ("At the end of the day, it's about people.").

**Fix:** cut the transition and see if the sentences still follow; they usually do. Swap the adjective for the fact that made it true ("crucial" becomes "without it, checkout fails"). End a paragraph on its last real point, not on a summary of it.

## Words and phrases that read generic

More examples for the clues above, by kind. For a longer, tiered list with suggested swaps, see [SlopDetector's word list](https://slopdetector.org/blog/ai-words-list) (2026). Lists like this go stale, so check the current one rather than copying it.

- **Big words for small actions:** "delve", "leverage", "utilize", "embark". Usually "look at", "use", "start".
- **More hype adjectives** (see "Generic claims"): "cutting-edge", "comprehensive", "innovative".
- **More stock openers:** "Let's dive into", "When it comes to", "Picture this".
- **The staged reveal:** "The result?", "Here's the thing", "And the best part?". One can be a voice; one per paragraph is a tic.
- **Glue pairs that mean nothing:** "ever-evolving landscape", "holistic approach", "multifaceted approach".
- **Encyclopedia voice:** "is defined as", "plays an important role in". The reader came for your case, not a definition.
- **Business-speak:** "move the needle", "low-hanging fruit", "take it to the next level".

**A word on the list is a clue, not proof.** SlopDetector's page says it too: "A single word is almost never proof of anything." Density is the tell, and one "crucial" in a long post is just a word. Words the author chose on purpose are part of the voice. And a find-and-replace doesn't fix generic text: swapping "leverage" for "use" leaves the sentence as empty as before. The finding still needs a place in the work and a concrete change, usually a fact, a number or a cut.

## The fix has two parts

Every text finding ends with **a concrete change** (the rewritten line, the cut, the fact to add) and, when the fix needs something only the author knows, **one honest open question**. "What did it cost?" is a good question. "Consider adding more detail" is not a finding.
